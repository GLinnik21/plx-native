# P2H — what a rung declares, what it delivers, and what it costs, on a real PMS

**Host-only. No television, no lease.** Reproduce with `tools/pms-rung-sweep.py`, which drives
`tools/pms-hls-probe.py` once per rung and pairs the segment byte lists by media index. Every
table below is generated by that tool from the `segments.csv` files beside this document; none of
it is transcribed.

Measured 2026-08-27 against PMS 1.43.3.10896. Four windows over three library items:

| run | item shape | window | rungs | segments |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|
| `movie/opening` | 1080p h264, 2.39:1 | 0–80 s | 13 | 520 |
| `movie/40min` | the same item | 2400–2480 s | 13 | 520 |
| `4k-hevc/40min` | 4K HEVC, high bitrate | 2400–2480 s | 5 | 200 |
| `episode/10min` | TV episode, h264 | 600–680 s | 5 | 200 |

## Why this was host-only work when the plan filed it as a device job

The adaptive-playback plan ranks an **adjacent-pair byte census** among the seven cases needing a
45-minute television lease. It does not need one. The bytes are PMS's, and PMS hands them to any
HTTP client — so the SIZE half of the census is a host job, and only the TIMING half (transaction
cost, queue behaviour, the feed) needs the set. Doing it here also buys something a device run
could not: the same media indices at every rung, so a byte ratio is the actuator's effect rather
than the difference between two scenes.

## What this apparatus cannot answer, stated before the results

**Nothing about the link.** The configured PMS runs on the dev Mac. Measured body throughput
reaches 7 092 Mbit/s, so the transport leg is free and `tau` — the per-byte transfer cost — is
unmeasurable here. Any fit of it against this data would be reading noise. The device tier owns
that half, and this document makes no claim about it.

**Nothing about a whole film.** Each run samples one contiguous 80 s span. Two spans of the same
film differ by 4.3x in median segment rate (below), so no single window characterises an item.

**One server, one client profile.** The probe sends the same HLS transcode target the app does,
byte for byte (`plex/transcoder.rs:182-183`). It does **not** send the app's three
`add-limitation` clauses or its direct-play profile. For a transcode decision at 1920x1080 and
below those should not bind, but "should not" is an argument, not a measurement.

## 1. `BANDWIDTH` is the decision's own bitrate, exactly — and it is not the RFC 8216 peak

In the wire shape the app actually uses (`autoAdjustQuality=0`, `videoBitrate` = `peakBitrate` =
`maxVideoBitrate` = the rung), PMS returns a master playlist with **one** variant whose
`BANDWIDTH` equals the `/decision` response's `bitrate` field times 1000. **26 rung/window pairs
checked, 0 mismatches.**

That kills the plan's §6 lead in its stated form. RFC 8216 §4.3.4.2 requires `BANDWIDTH` to be the
**peak** segment bit rate; this server publishes the **target average**. The two differ, and §6 of
the plan hoped they would not:

> If PMS honours the spec, the size bound admission needs is **already in the manifest** and the
> byte census becomes verification rather than discovery.

It is in the manifest, but as a target rather than a peak — which is a weaker object, and the
next section is about how much weaker.

One shape difference worth recording because it inverts the answer: with `autoAdjustQuality=1` and
`peakBitrate=20000`, `BANDWIDTH` comes back as **20 000 000** — a verbatim echo of the request,
36.6x the decision's own 547 kbps, and `RESOLUTION` echoes the requested box rather than the
chosen raster. The app does not send that shape. A probe run that did would have concluded
`BANDWIDTH` is meaningless, which is the opposite of the truth.

## 2. What a rung delivers: `s`, and where its bound actually holds

Define `s = 8 * bytes / (duration * BANDWIDTH)` — the segment's delivered rate over its declared
rate. Dimensionless, so it is comparable across rungs in a way bytes are not.

**Does the declaration bound the delivery?** One row per rung, pooled over every window that
reached it. **The fifth column is new and it is the one that changed the answer** — the episode
run through the FULL ladder, which `docs/adaptive-playback-spec.md` §8 asked for first because
rung 2000 rested on a single film:

### [REFUTED] The `s <= 0.85` bound does not hold at rung 2000. It holds at 4000 and above.

An earlier version of this section read: **"At every rung at or above 2000 kbps, not one of 1 120
segments exceeded 0.85x its declared `BANDWIDTH`."** That sentence was true of the corpus that
produced it and is **false**. The episode, run through the full ladder, puts **9 of 40** segments
at rung 2000 above `0.85 * W`, to a maximum of **0.9175**.

This is exactly the hole the specification predicted, in the place it predicted: rung 2000 is the
boundary of the admission rule, eight of the eleven rungs at or above it rested on one film, and
the one item that already broke the bound one rung lower was absent from 2000 entirely. It was
named as the first thing to do and it refuted the claim on the first run. **The corrected
statement is `s <= 0.85` for rungs at or above 4000** — 0 of **1 440** segments, max **0.8456**.

The failure is not a surprise once the mechanism is written down. Max `s` decays **monotonically**
with the rung — 1.285, 1.155, 0.917, 0.846, 0.837, 0.819, 0.820, 0.799, 0.805, 0.808, 0.804,
0.804, 0.798 — which is one curve, not a bound that holds and then breaks. The encoder cannot go
below a content-dependent **quality floor**, so at a small enough rate target the floor wins and
the segment overshoots. Rung 2000 is simply where that curve crosses 0.85 on this item.

The two lowest rungs are the same mechanism at full strength: at rung 320 the server targets
180 kbps of video and the peak segment carries 1.91x that. **That is a property of the bottom of
the ladder, not of the encoder in general.**

**The median is not the max, and the difference is the whole reason the max is interesting.**
Median `s` moves enormously with content while the max barely moves at all — the same item, two
80 s windows:

| rung | median `s`, opening | median `s`, 40 min | ratio | max, opening | max, 40 min |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 320 | 0.864 | 1.054 | 1.22 | 1.075 | 1.135 |
| 720 | 0.619 | 0.928 | 1.50 | 0.942 | 0.962 |
| 2000 | 0.275 | 0.786 | 2.86 | 0.809 | 0.842 |
| 4000 | 0.261 | 0.763 | 2.92 | 0.846 | 0.835 |
| 8000 | 0.193 | 0.724 | 3.74 | 0.811 | 0.819 |
| 12000 | 0.164 | 0.679 | 4.14 | 0.799 | 0.782 |
| 18000 | 0.143 | 0.611 | 4.26 | 0.804 | 0.779 |
| 22000 | 0.153 | 0.644 | 4.20 | 0.798 | 0.786 |

A 4.3x swing in the median against a max that holds inside `[0.77, 0.85]` is the signature of a
**cap**, not of a distribution that happened to be sampled twice. That is what makes the bound
usable: it is not a high quantile of a shifting distribution, it is a ceiling the encoder does not
cross.

## 2a. `s` is a per-rung quantity, and the margin for an unseen item is measurable

The refutation above kills the single-constant form of `s`, not the quantity. What replaces it is
the same measurement read per rung instead of pooled across rungs — and, separately, an answer to
the question the single constant could never answer: **how much does an item this server has never
transcoded move the bound?**

That is answerable here because five item-windows now reach the same rungs. Per rung, take each
window's own max `s` and compare the largest to the smallest:

| rung | 4k-hevc | episode/10m | episode/full | movie/40m | movie/open | cross-item spread |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 320 | 1.080 | 1.285 | 1.285 | 1.135 | 1.075 | **1.196** |
| 720 | 0.992 | 1.155 | 1.155 | 0.962 | 0.942 | **1.227** |
| 2000 | — | — | 0.917 | 0.842 | 0.809 | **1.134** |
| 4000 | 0.801 | 0.843 | 0.843 | 0.835 | 0.846 | 1.055 |
| 6000 | — | — | 0.837 | 0.818 | 0.801 | 1.045 |
| 8000 | — | — | 0.818 | 0.819 | 0.811 | 1.010 |
| 10000 | — | — | 0.819 | 0.792 | 0.820 | 1.036 |
| 12000 | 0.757 | 0.797 | 0.797 | 0.782 | 0.799 | 1.056 |
| 14000 | — | — | 0.794 | 0.790 | 0.805 | 1.018 |
| 16000 | — | — | 0.798 | 0.770 | 0.808 | 1.049 |
| 18000 | — | — | 0.788 | 0.779 | 0.804 | 1.032 |
| 20000 | — | — | 0.795 | 0.779 | 0.804 | 1.032 |
| 22000 | 0.757 | 0.785 | 0.785 | 0.786 | 0.798 | 1.054 |

**The spread splits the ladder in the same place the refutation did.** At every rung at or above
4000 an unseen item moved the max by at most **5.6%**. At 2000 it moves **13.4%**, at 720 **22.7%**
and at 320 **19.6%** — the quality-floor regime is not merely higher, it is far more
item-dependent, which is what makes a shared constant wrong there specifically.

**So the margin is derived rather than chosen.** Ship, per rung, the pooled max scaled by the
largest cross-item ratio observed at rungs where the ratio is stable:

```
sigma_j  =  max_observed(j)  x  spread(j)
```

Both factors are (2) directly measured; the product is (3) mathematically derived. For rungs >= 4000
that is `0.846 x 1.056 = 0.893`, i.e. **`sigma = 0.90`** — which is the first version of this number
with any margin at all. The pooled max is 0.8456, so shipping 0.85 leaves **0.5%**, and 0.5% is not
a safety margin, it is a rounding artefact of the measurement that produced it.

**Two things this does NOT establish, stated because the refutation above is what happens when they
are forgotten.** The spread is computed over five windows on one server and three items; it bounds
the sixth only under the assumption that item-to-item variation is exchangeable, which is precisely
the assumption rung 2000 just violated at the pooled level. And every window is 80 s of one
programme. **`sigma` must therefore be verified against every fetched segment at run time** — the
app already logs `bytes=` — and a rule that depends on it has to degrade when it is exceeded rather
than treat exceedance as impossible. A bound measured on five windows is a seed, not a guarantee.

## 3. The relative bound does **not** survive

The tempting alternative — predict the candidate's bytes by scaling the bytes just measured at the
current rung by the ratio of the two declared rates — is **refuted**. Paired by media index, the
delivered ratio exceeds the declared ratio in every run:

| run | worst step | violations | worst overshoot |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| movie/40min | 12000->14000 | 36/40 | 1.180 |
| movie/40min | 2000->4000 | 13/40 | 1.139 |
| episode/10min | 12000->22000 | 3/40 | 1.067 |
| 4k-hevc/40min | 12000->22000 | 5/40 | 1.011 |

So the declared ratio is not a bound on the delivered ratio, even though the declared rate is a
bound on the delivered rate at rungs above 2000. Both statements are measurements; only the first
one is available to an admission rule.

## 4. Segment size has no stable memory — a prediction of mine, refuted

The `movie/opening` window is strongly autocorrelated at lag 1 (`rho1` 0.64–0.76 at every rung,
Ljung-Box Q(5) 20.7–39.8 against a 11.07 critical value), and on that window alone I wrote down
that real content persists and that the plan's order-statistic bound therefore needs an AR(1)
correction with `n_eff = n(1-rho)/(1+rho)`.

**The other three windows refute it.** Same measurement, same tool:

| run | 320 | 720 | 4000 | 12000 | 22000 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| movie/opening | 0.764 | 0.723 | 0.655 | 0.668 | 0.657 |
| movie/40min | -0.230 | 0.311 | -0.198 | 0.179 | 0.250 |
| 4k-hevc/40min | 0.288 | 0.108 | -0.306 | -0.286 | -0.287 |
| episode/10min | -0.376 | -0.314 | 0.004 | 0.167 | 0.215 |

`rho1` spans **-0.376 to +0.764** with no consistent sign, by rung or by run. It is a property of
the particular eighty seconds, not of content. **No fixed `rho` is estimable, so no AR(1)
correction is justified** — and equally, exchangeability is not established either. A statistical
bound on segment SIZE rests on an assumption this data will not supply in either direction.

That is an argument for §2's structural bound rather than a statistical one, and it is worth being
explicit about the direction of the argument: the structural bound was not chosen because it is
tidier. It is the only one of the three candidates that survived being tested.

## 5. Cost: a 108 ms serving quantum, and a control plane that is not free

**Time to first byte is quantized.** Over all **1 426** segment fetches above 50 ms, the best-fit
quantum is **108.13 ms**, with a median residual of **3.47 ms** (p90 8.36). If time to first byte
were continuous and unrelated to that grid the median residual would be ~27 ms — so the fit is
7.8x tighter than chance. The multiples used are overwhelmingly 1, 2 and 3.

This is a serving cost, not production, and two independent legs say so:

* **Pacing does not change it.** 25 segments pulled at one every 2.0 s — far slower than the
  encoder produces — give a median of 213.4 ms against 214.6 ms unpaced. If the client were
  waiting for the encoder, getting out of its way would have shown.
* **It does not scale with the rung on an easy source.** On `movie/opening` the median is
  211–215 ms from rung 2000 to rung 22000, a 69x range of declared bitrate and a 12x range of
  segment bytes.

**It becomes production on a hard source.** The 4K HEVC item is the exception that locates the
boundary — median time to first byte by rung: 160, 112, 222, 426, **862** ms, max **1 306** ms.
There the transcoder genuinely is the constraint, and the cost rises with the rung, which is
exactly where the budget is tightest.

**A seek costs one expensive segment and no more.** Requesting an index the encoder has not
reached (index 900 and 1800 of a session that had produced 4 segments) cost **654 ms** and
**663 ms** — about 6 quanta. The very next segment came back in 111 ms and 208 ms. The relocation
is real, bounded, and does not persist.

**The control plane** is three requests, and the expensive one is not the one the plan's R8
assumed. Warm, per transaction: `/decision` **13–18 ms**, `start.m3u8` **9–15 ms**, the media
playlist **88–115 ms** — total **118 ms** at the medians, dominated by a 114 KB, 4 235-entry playlist
that is refetched in full on every rung change. The *first* `/decision` for an item costs
**737 ms** cold; every later one is warm.

## 6. The ladder's top is degenerate, and the catalog's own numbers are wrong

`HlsActuatorCatalog::measured()` (`abr/ladder.rs:185-210`) stores an `expected_wire_kbps` per
rung, documented as "what the server was measured to DO with it". Measured against this server:

| rung | catalog `expected_wire_kbps` | declared, this item | error |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 320 | 320 | 303 | +5.6% |
| 720 | 720 | 547 | +31.6% |
| 2000 | 2 000 | 1 830 | +9.3% |
| 4000 | 4 000 | 3 775 | +6.0% |
| 6000 | 6 000 | 5 696 | +5.3% |
| 8000 | 8 000 | 7 515 | +6.5% |
| 10000 | 10 000 | 9 448 | +5.8% |
| 12000 | 12 000 | 11 378 | +5.5% |
| 14000 | 14 000 | 13 311 | +5.2% |
| 16000 | 16 000 | 15 126 | +5.8% |
| 18000 | 18 000 | 16 150 | +11.5% |
| 20011 | 20 011 | **16 150** | **+23.9%** |
| 20895 | 20 895 | 20 895 | exact |

Three separate findings sit in that table.

**(a) The value is item-dependent and the table is a constant.** Rung 720 declares 547 on the
movie and 425 on the other two items — a 29% spread. Rung 22000 declares 20 895, 20 895 and
20 379. A per-item quantity stored as a per-server constant will be wrong for most items.

**(b) The `P1080High` entry was measured under a request the app never makes.** Its 20 011 comes
from `docs/pms-hls-protocol-probe.md`'s ceiling table, taken with a **3840x2160** ceiling.
`Rung::P1080High::raster()` is `(1920, 1080)`, so the app sends a 1080p ceiling and gets 16 150.
I first hypothesised that the ceiling box itself unlocks the higher rate, and **tested it: it does
not.** 20 000 kbps returns 16 150 with either box. The step boundary from the old doc does
reproduce — 21 750 gives 16 150, 21 999 gives 20 895 — so what moved is the value below the
boundary, which means the old 20 011 was that item's number, not this server's.

**(c) Rungs 18000 and 20000 are the same stream.** Both declare 16 150, and **39 of 40 segments
are byte-identical by sha256, at both windows.** This is stronger than R15's "quality tie": it is
not that the two rungs look alike, it is that requesting the dearer one returns the cheaper one's
bytes. The controller holds two budgets, two production-load figures and two ladder positions for
one encoder session.

## 6b. M3: the production ladder is the same per-item constant, and it is inert

The plan's step **M3** asks for the acquisition ratio `rho = total_fetch_us / media_duration_ms`
per rung, cold and warm, against a 4K and a 1080p source, in order to re-derive
`production_load_pm`. The sweep already carries it. Medians:

| rung | movie/opening | movie/40min | 4k-hevc/40min | episode/10min | catalog `load_pm` |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 320 | 0.159 | 0.160 | 0.082 | 0.166 | 90 |
| 720 | 0.160 | 0.163 | 0.058 | 0.163 | 180 |
| 2000 | 0.110 | 0.162 | — | — | 420 |
| 4000 | 0.110 | 0.113 | 0.112 | 0.163 | 450 |
| 6000 | 0.110 | 0.108 | — | — | 900 |
| 8000 | 0.110 | 0.107 | — | — | 930 |
| 10000 | 0.108 | 0.057 | — | — | 950 |
| 12000 | 0.108 | 0.057 | **0.215** | 0.163 | 970 |
| 14000 | 0.109 | 0.057 | — | — | 980 |
| 16000 | 0.109 | 0.055 | — | — | 990 |
| 18000 | 0.110 | 0.056 | — | — | 995 |
| 20000 | 0.110 | 0.057 | — | — | 1000 |
| 22000 | 0.109 | 0.057 | **0.430** | 0.162 | 2100 |

**The catalog's two anchor measurements reproduce, and reproduce on exactly one of the four
runs.** Its doc claims "the 1080p high point produced segments at a 0.21 production ratio and the
4K point at 0.44". On the 4K HEVC source: **0.215 and 0.430**. On the 1080p source: **0.108 and
0.109** — flat, and equal to each other. So `production_load_pm`'s 23x spread from 90 to 2100 is
a 4K source's cost curve, applied to every item.

**What `rho` is actually measuring here has to be said, because it is not production.** On the
easy sources `rho` is just the serving quantum divided by the segment duration: 0.110 is 214 ms
over 2 000, i.e. two quanta, and the 0.057 column is one quantum. It reflects encoder work only
where production exceeds the quantum, which happens on the 4K item at rungs 12000 and above. The
right reading of the table is `rho ≈ max(quanta * 108 ms, production) / D` — and for the modal
item the first term wins at every rung.

**That makes the model inert exactly as the plan suspected, and now says when it stops being so.**
`predicted_ratio_pm` (`abr/plant.rs:113-121`) scales only the part of the measured ratio above
`production_floor_pm`, which is **250** (`abr/mod.rs:159`). Measured `rho` on the 1080p item is
108–110 per mille at every one of the thirteen rungs, so `work` is zero and the prediction returns
the measurement unchanged whatever the candidate — the entire `production_load_pm` column has no
effect. It engages only on the 4K item at 12000 (215, still below the floor) and 22000 (430, above
it). One item in four, one rung in thirteen.

## 7. What this changes

Confirmed, and now with a mechanism:

* **R1 stands and is sharpened.** The admission rule must not use catalog rung rates. But the
  replacement it needed — measured per-rung byte ratios from a device census — is not the answer
  either (§3). The answer is the server's declared rate, which §2 shows bounds delivery at rungs
  **4000 and above** — not 2000, as this line said until the episode refuted it — and which the
  transaction **already fetches and already logs**
  (`ff.rs:2660`, `hls: master one-variant bandwidth=`) before deciding anything.
* **R2's unreachable top gains a second, independent cause.** Even with the queue resized, two of
  the ladder's top rungs are the same encoder session on a 1080p item.
* **R8 is half right.** The control plane is indeed three requests, not a near-zero transfer —
  but the cost is in the media playlist, not `/decision`.
* **The plan's "inert argmax" reading of the production model** (§7.A, N19), now with the
  measurement under it and a boundary: inert for the modal item at every rung, live only on a 4K
  source at the top (§6b).

Closed without a television, against the plan's own ranking:

* **Case 3, the adjacent-pair byte census** — the plan's third-ranked device case. Done here, on
  three items, paired by media index rather than by rung average.
* **Step M3**, which the plan already filed as host work, is answered in §6b — including its
  paced-vs-back-to-back leg, which turns out not to matter on this apparatus (§5).
* **The `BANDWIDTH` lead**, which the plan said to check *before* building the census. Checking it
  first would not have saved the census, but it does replace what the census was for.

Refuted:

* **The RFC 8216 lead**, in its stated form (§1).
* **The relative byte-ratio bound** (§3), which was the shape I expected to find.
* **A stable segment-size autocorrelation** (§4), which I asserted from one window before the
  other three contradicted it.

Newly open, and none of it needs a television:

* **A second SERVER.** The per-rung `sigma` of §2a is measured on one server across three items and
  five windows. That is now enough items to *derive* a cross-item margin (§2a), which is what the
  second item bought; it is still one server, one transcoder build and one client profile.
* **Rungs 320, 720 and 2000 have no usable ceiling** and must not be admitted through the declared
  rate at all. §2a gives their measured values, but with 13-23% cross-item spread they are seeds
  for a run-time estimate, not bounds.
* **Whether `sigma` needs to be a shipped table at all.** An upshift needs a size prediction; a
  DOWNSHIFT does not, because acquisition time cannot rise when the byte count falls. If the
  controller only ever predicts sizes upward, the three floor-regime rungs stop mattering, since
  they are downshift targets.
* Why 108 ms.
* Whether the 320 and 720 rungs' overshoot is bounded at all, or merely was here.
